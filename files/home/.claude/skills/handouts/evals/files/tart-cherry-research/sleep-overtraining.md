# Research: Tart cherry juice for sleep, fatigue, and overreaching/overtraining in athletes

## Summary
Tart cherry juice/concentrate has small, plausible evidence for improving sleep duration/efficiency and modest evidence for improving short-term recovery from muscle-damaging or prolonged exercise. I found **no direct evidence** that tart cherry treats non-functional overreaching or overtraining syndrome (OTS); relevance to “overtraining fatigue” is indirect via sleep, soreness, inflammation/oxidative-stress markers, and acute recovery. Suspected OTS should be managed as a training/medical problem, not a supplement problem.

## Findings
1. **Sleep evidence is positive but small and not overtraining-specific.** In a randomized crossover trial, 7 days of Montmorency tart cherry concentrate increased urinary melatonin metabolite and improved actigraphy sleep time/efficiency in healthy adults. Older-adult insomnia pilot trials also reported modest improvements, including a small PSG-based trial reporting longer sleep time, but samples were tiny and mostly non-athletes. [Howatson 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22038497/); [Pigeon 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20438325/); [Losso 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28901958/)

2. **Exercise-recovery evidence is stronger than sleep evidence, but it targets acute damage/fatigue, not OTS.** Trials around eccentric exercise, marathon running, relay running, and repeated high-intensity cycling generally show less soreness/pain, faster strength recovery, or lower inflammatory/oxidative-stress markers with tart cherry juice/concentrate. These are short protocols around a damaging event or heavy block, not athletes with persistent performance decrement lasting weeks/months. [Connolly 2006](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2579450/); [Kuehl 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2874510/); [Howatson 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19883392/); [Bell 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3942735/)

3. **No direct overtraining/overreaching treatment evidence was identified.** The available tart-cherry athlete studies measure DOMS, muscle function, inflammatory markers, oxidative stress, endurance performance, or sleep. Those endpoints may matter during intensified training, but they do not establish benefit for non-functional overreaching or OTS as defined by sustained performance decrement and delayed recovery after rest. [Meeusen consensus](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23247672/); [cherry evidence review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5872786/)

4. **Consensus definition matters: OTS is retrospective and a diagnosis of exclusion.** The ECSS/ACSM consensus distinguishes functional overreaching, non-functional overreaching, and OTS mainly by performance decrement and recovery time: days-weeks for functional overreaching, weeks-months for non-functional overreaching, and months for OTS. There is no single diagnostic biomarker; clinicians should exclude anemia/iron deficiency, infection, endocrine disease, low energy availability/REDs, depression, sleep disorders, and other medical causes. [Meeusen 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23247672/); [Kreher & Schwartz 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3435910/)

5. **Management consensus: reduce load, recover, and investigate causes.** Recommended management is rest or major training-load reduction, adequate energy/carbohydrate/protein intake, sleep restoration, psychological-stress reduction, and gradual return only after symptoms and performance normalize. Monitoring should prioritize performance trends, mood/wellness, sleep, illness/injury frequency, and training load rather than relying on one lab marker. [Meeusen 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23247672/); [Halson 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4213373/)

6. **Red flags needing sports-medicine evaluation.** Persistent fatigue or declining performance despite 1-2+ weeks of deload/rest, recurrent illness, unexplained weight loss, disordered eating, menstrual dysfunction/low libido, stress fractures or recurrent bone injury, resting tachycardia/palpitations, syncope/chest pain, fever/night sweats, severe mood symptoms, or insomnia that does not respond to basic sleep intervention should prompt medical review. REDs/low energy availability is especially important because it can present as fatigue, poor recovery, mood/sleep disturbance, injury, and performance decline. [IOC REDs 2023](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/57/17/1073); [Kreher & Schwartz 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3435910/)

## Practical implications
- **Reasonable use case:** short-term adjunct around competitions, eccentric/damaging sessions, or heavy blocks where soreness and sleep are limiting. Common study dosing: ~30 mL Montmorency tart cherry concentrate twice daily, or ~240-355 mL juice twice daily, for ~4-7 days before and 2-4 days after an event; insomnia studies used ~1-2 weeks.
- **Do not treat it as an OTS therapy.** If the athlete has persistent fatigue and performance decline, the primary intervention is deload/rest plus evaluation of sleep, fueling, iron status, illness, endocrine issues, mental health, and REDs.
- **Potential downsides:** extra sugar/calories, GI upset, cost, and uncertain value for athletes already sleeping well. Chronic high-dose antioxidant/polyphenol use may be best reserved for recovery/competition periods rather than every training day if the goal is maximizing adaptation.
- **Best framing:** tart cherry may improve the recovery environment; it does not fix an excessive load-recovery mismatch.

## Gaps
- No direct tart-cherry trials in athletes diagnosed with non-functional overreaching or OTS were identified.
- Most tart-cherry studies are small, short-term, and use heterogeneous products/doses; exact product standardization matters.
- Search tooling was unavailable during this run, so if decisions depend on the newest literature, run a final PubMed/SPORTDiscus check for 2025-2026 tart-cherry + overreaching/overtraining studies.
